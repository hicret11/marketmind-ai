## Definition

Segmentation is splitting a broad market into groups that share a meaningful trait — a need, a behavior, a buying trigger — so you can message and target each group more precisely than a one-size-fits-all approach.

## Why it matters

"Parents" is not a segment you can market to effectively. "Parents planning a child's 5th–8th birthday party in the next 60 days" is. The more specific and behaviorally meaningful the segment, the sharper the message and the higher the conversion.

:::key-idea
Good segments are defined by what predicts behavior (intent, occasion, role, pain point) — not just who someone is (age, gender, job title) on its own.
:::

## How it works

Common segmentation axes:

- **Demographic/firmographic** — age, income, company size, industry.
- **Behavioral** — past purchases, engagement level, usage frequency.
- **Needs-based** — what problem they're trying to solve right now.
- **Occasion-based** — a specific moment that triggers demand (a birthday, a launch, a renewal).

For B2B specifically, segment by role (owner vs. events manager vs. marketing lead) — each has a different buying trigger.

## Framework

1. List the different "jobs" your product gets hired for.
2. For each job, describe the segment: who, what triggers the need, what they currently do instead.
3. Rank segments by size × ease of reach × willingness to pay.
4. Build messaging per segment — don't force one message across all of them.

:::example
Sing My Birthday has at least two very different segments: (1) individual gift-buyers looking for a unique present, and (2) venue owners looking for a premium add-on to sell inside existing packages. Same product, completely different message and channel.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Segmenting only by demographics when the real driver is occasion or intent.
- Creating so many segments that none gets enough focused effort.
- Building a "persona" from imagination instead of real customer research.

:::mistake
Treating a B2B buyer and a B2C buyer with the same content and channel strategy. Their research process, decision timeline and trust signals are fundamentally different.
:::

## Metrics to track

- Conversion rate by segment (not just in aggregate)
- Customer acquisition cost by segment
- Which segment has the shortest path to a repeat purchase or referral
