## Definition

A/B testing (split testing) is comparing two versions of something — an ad, a page, a subject line — by showing each to a separate audience segment and measuring which performs better against a defined metric.

## Why it matters

Opinions about what will "work better" are frequently wrong, even for experienced marketers. A/B testing replaces debate with evidence, and compounds over time as small wins stack up.

:::key-idea
A test is only valid if you change one variable at a time and let it run until you have enough data — otherwise you can't attribute the result to the thing you changed.
:::

## How it works

A clean test needs:

- A single variable changed (headline, image, price, subject line).
- A clearly defined success metric decided before the test starts.
- Enough sample size/time to reach a meaningful result, not just an early lead.
- A control group unaffected by the change, for fair comparison.

## Framework

1. Form a specific hypothesis ("changing the headline to X will increase conversion because Y").
2. Change only that one variable between version A and B.
3. Run until you hit a pre-defined sample size or time window — don't stop early just because one side is ahead.
4. Document the result and the reasoning, win or lose — losing tests are still useful data.

:::example
Testing two ad headlines — "Personalized Birthday Songs" vs. "A Birthday Song With Their Name In It" — with identical creative and targeting isolates which specific wording drives a better click-through and conversion rate.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Testing multiple variables at once, making results impossible to interpret.
- Calling a test "won" too early, based on a tiny difference in a small sample.
- Never testing again after the first result — testing is an ongoing habit, not a one-off event.

:::mistake
Ending a test the moment one variant looks ahead, without reaching statistical or practical significance — early leads frequently reverse with more data.
:::

## Metrics to track

- Primary success metric defined before the test (not chosen afterward to fit the result)
- Sample size / statistical confidence
- Practical significance (is the lift big enough to matter, not just measurable?)
